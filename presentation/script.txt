Title slide (about 1 min)
- Good afternoon, and thank you for being here.
- This presentation asks a simple but important question: when a border is still geographically close, but crossings become unavailable, what happens to local economic activity?
- The key idea I want you to keep in mind is that access is not straight-line distance; it is road-network access to a crossing that is actually open.
- I focus on the Colombia-Venezuela border because restrictions after 2019 created a clear change in effective accessibility.

Motivation (about 2 min)
- Border closures can affect daily economic life through trade, commuting, services, and local business linkages.
- In many border studies, treatment is often binary (near/far from border), but that misses the fact that road detours can vary a lot even within the same border region.
- Here the motivation is to capture that variation directly: not everyone near the border faces the same shock.
- Nighttime lights are useful because they provide consistent quarterly coverage where conventional local output data are limited.
- The takeaway from this slide is: the relevant shock is a change in usable connectivity, not only administrative border status.

Introduction (about 2 min)
- Research question: how did changes in road-network access to the Colombia-Venezuela border affect local economic activity?
- Contribution 1: a continuous exposure measure based on shortest-path access to operational crossings.
- Contribution 2: a municipality-quarter panel combining GIS infrastructure and VIIRS nighttime lights.
- Contribution 3: a border-specific empirical setting where access changed sharply, allowing stronger within-area comparisons.
- I also want to emphasize why this matters: two municipalities with similar geography can face different economic pressure once actual routes and crossing availability are considered.

Conceptual framework (about 2 min)
- The mechanism has three linked pieces.
- First, policy shock: closures and reopenings change which crossings can be used.
- Second, transmission: routes become longer or more costly, weakening cross-border interactions.
- Third, outcome: weaker accessibility should appear as lower local nighttime-light activity.
- Expected sign: larger access losses should be associated with weaker activity.
- Literature comparision:
  - Border and trade costs: Anderson & van Wincoop (2003), Brulhart et al. (2012), Heiland et al. (2019).
  - Transport infrastructure and access: Redding & Turner (2015), Donaldson (2018), Banerjee et al. (2020).
  - Remote sensing and nighttime lights: Henderson et al. (2012), Pinkovskiy & Sala-i-Martin (2016), Beyer et al. (2022).
- Framing sentence to close this slide: this project sits at the intersection of these three strands by linking policy-driven access shocks to satellite-observed local outcomes.

Methodology: empirical pipeline (about 2 min)
- Step 1: build the motorcar road network and connect municipalities to the border-crossing system.
- Step 2: compute shortest-path travel costs/times to the nearest operational crossing.
- Step 3: convert pre/post access differences into municipality-quarter treatment intensity.
- Step 4: estimate panel models with fixed effects and dynamic checks.
- Important emphasis: treatment intensity is continuous, which helps avoid forcing very different municipalities into the same treated/control bucket.

Methodology: baseline model and specifications (about 2 min)
- The dependent variable is log(1 + nighttime lights) at municipality-quarter level.
- Municipality fixed effects absorb time-invariant local differences.
- Time fixed effects absorb common quarter shocks.
- The coefficient of interest captures how increases in access loss relate to nighttime-light changes.
- Then I move through a specification ladder:
  - TWFE baseline,
  - Country x quarter fixed effects,
  - Crossing corridor x quarter fixed effects,
  - State/department x quarter fixed effects.
- Identification logic: if estimates persist under tighter localized controls, they are less likely to reflect confounding regional shocks.

Data (about 2 min)
- Five layers are combined:
  - Municipality boundaries (units of analysis),
  - OpenStreetMap motorcar roads (network structure),
  - Georeferenced crossings and their status timeline,
  - VIIRS nighttime lights (quarterly outcome),
  - Final sample size: 192 municipalities and 4,032 municipality-quarter observations.
- Coverage runs from 2012Q2 to 2025Q4.
- Use the map to remind the audience this is a network-measured accessibility design, not a simple border strip comparison.

Results: main estimates (about 2 min)
- Baseline TWFE estimate is negative and statistically significant: -0.0168 (p = 0.033).
- With country x quarter controls, magnitude shrinks and significance weakens: -0.0104 (p = 0.146).
- With tighter local controls (state/department x quarter), estimate is small and imprecise: -0.0057 (p = 0.616).
- With municipality trends, effect is near zero.
- Main interpretation: the initial negative relationship is present, but not robust to stricter control structures.

Results: dynamic check (about 2 min)
- Event-study profiles show differential pre-trends.
- This means treated and less-treated areas were not moving in fully parallel ways before the major disruption.
- With more localized controls, dynamic effects weaken further, though patterns do not fully vanish.
- Core message: dynamic evidence supports caution and prevents over-claiming a clean causal estimate.

Results: reopening and heterogeneity (about 2 min)
- Reopening evidence is the strongest mechanism-consistent result.
- Municipalities that had larger earlier access losses tend to recover more when access improves.
- Heterogeneity:
  - Colombia coefficient appears positive and borderline significant.
  - Venezuela coefficient appears negative but statistically imprecise.
- So heterogeneity is suggestive, not definitive; I present it as descriptive support rather than a final causal split.

Conclusion (about 1.5 min)
- Bottom line: border accessibility appears to matter, but the strongest claim is supportive evidence, not definitive causal proof.
- Larger access losses are associated with weaker nighttime-light activity in border municipalities.
- The effect is sensitive to tighter local time controls, so causal language should remain cautious.
- The reopening pattern is the most compelling mechanism-consistent piece of evidence.
- Close with: this design is still useful because it quantifies access shocks in a way binary border treatments cannot.

Things I should be careful to clarify
- Clarify that exposure is a continuous road-network accessibility change, not a binary treated indicator.
- Clarify that nighttime lights are a proxy for local activity, not a direct measure of GDP.
- Clarify that differential pre-trends limit strong causal interpretation of TWFE estimates.
- Clarify why progressively tighter fixed effects are necessary in this setting.
- Clarify that reopening evidence supports the mechanism but does not eliminate all confounding concerns.
- Clarify that heterogeneity results are imprecise and should not be over-interpreted.

Things that might ask and their answer
- Q: Why use nighttime lights instead of conventional economic indicators?
  A: Municipal-quarter conventional indicators are sparse/inconsistent here; VIIRS provides harmonized, high-frequency spatial coverage for the full panel period.
- Q: Why not use a simple treated vs. untreated border dummy?
  A: A dummy masks large variation in actual detours; two nearby municipalities can face very different route shocks after closures.
- Q: How do you address national shocks like inflation or macro instability?
  A: By adding country x quarter fixed effects, then corridor x quarter and state/department x quarter fixed effects to absorb progressively more localized time-varying shocks.
- Q: What is the strongest evidence in your results?
  A: The reopening pattern, where larger prior access losses are associated with stronger recovery when access improves.
- Q: Are your estimates causal?
  A: I present them as cautious, supportive evidence. Differential pre-trends in event studies mean causal claims should be restrained.
- Q: Why do coefficients shrink in stricter specifications?
  A: Tighter controls absorb more confounding local dynamics; that is exactly why the specification ladder is informative about robustness.
- Q: Do low within R2 values invalidate the findings?
  A: No. With fixed effects and quarterly local variation, low within R2 is common; interpretation relies on coefficient stability/significance across specifications.
- Q: What is the main policy insight?
  A: Border policy affects local economies through network accessibility. Maintaining or restoring usable crossings can matter for local recovery.
